COMMON PESTS AND DISEASES

Spider mites: Tiny arachnids, often too small to see clearly without a
magnifying glass, that thrive in warm, dry conditions. Signs include fine
webbing between leaves and stems, small pale or yellow speckling on leaves,
and an overall dusty or stippled appearance. Increasing humidity and
periodically rinsing leaves under running water helps discourage them;
insecticidal soap or neem oil are common treatments for active infestations.

Aphids: Small, soft-bodied insects, often green, black, or brown, that
cluster on new growth, stems, and flower buds. They excrete a sticky substance
called honeydew, which can attract ants or lead to sooty mold growth on
leaves. A strong stream of water, insecticidal soap, or introducing natural
predators like ladybugs can help control them.

Mealybugs: Small insects covered in a white, cottony wax, often found in leaf
joints and along stems. They can be treated by dabbing directly with rubbing
alcohol on a cotton swab, followed by a thorough rinse, repeated over a couple
of weeks to catch newly hatched insects.

Powdery mildew: A fungal disease appearing as a white or gray powdery coating
on leaves and stems, common in humid conditions with poor air circulation.
Improving airflow, avoiding wetting the foliage when watering, and removing
heavily affected leaves all help manage it.

Root rot: Caused by prolonged waterlogged soil, which starves roots of
oxygen and invites fungal pathogens. Affected roots often turn brown or black
and feel mushy rather than firm and white. Treatment involves removing
affected roots, repotting into fresh well-draining soil, and adjusting future
watering habits.

Leaf spot diseases: A broad category of fungal or bacterial infections that
cause discrete brown, black, or tan spots on foliage, sometimes with a yellow
halo. They often spread in wet, humid conditions, so watering at the soil
level rather than overhead, and removing affected leaves promptly, helps limit
spread.

General pest management: Isolating a newly acquired or visibly affected plant
from others for a couple of weeks helps prevent pests or disease from
spreading through a whole collection. Regularly inspecting the undersides of
leaves and new growth catches most problems early, when they're easiest to
treat.
