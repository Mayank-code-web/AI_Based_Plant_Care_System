LIGHT AND PLACEMENT FOR HOUSEPLANTS

Light is one of the most commonly misjudged factors in plant care, because
human eyes adapt to a wide range of brightness, making dim rooms feel brighter
than they actually are to a plant.

Light categories: "Low light" describes areas a few feet from a window, or
north-facing windows, where a plant receives ambient brightness but no direct
sun. "Medium indirect light" describes a spot near an east or west window, or
a few feet back from a bright south-facing window. "Bright indirect light"
describes being close to a sunny window without direct rays hitting the
leaves. "Direct sunlight" means the sun's rays fall directly on the foliage
for at least part of the day.

Signs a plant needs more light: Leggy, stretched-out stems with unusually
long gaps between leaves (etiolation), new leaves noticeably smaller than
older ones, leaning dramatically toward a light source, and slowed or stalled
growth. Variegated plants often lose their variegation and revert to solid
green under insufficient light.

Signs a plant is getting too much direct light: Pale, bleached, or scorched
patches on leaves (often brown or crispy with a defined edge), leaves that
feel unusually dry or crispy, and wilting even when the soil is adequately
moist.

Adjusting placement: When moving a plant to a brighter spot, do it gradually
over a week or two rather than all at once, since leaves that developed in low
light can sunburn if suddenly exposed to intense direct sun. Rotating a plant
a quarter turn every week or two helps it grow evenly instead of leaning
toward one side.

Artificial light: Full-spectrum LED grow lights can supplement or replace
natural light in low-light rooms. Most common houseplants do well with
12-16 hours of grow-light exposure per day, positioned close enough to be
effective but not so close that they generate excess heat on the leaves.
