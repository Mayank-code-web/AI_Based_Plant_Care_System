WATERING BASICS FOR HOUSEPLANTS AND GARDEN PLANTS

Most plant deaths in home gardening trace back to watering problems, not pests
or disease. The two extremes — overwatering and underwatering — often look
similar at first glance (drooping, yellowing leaves), which is why checking the
soil directly is more reliable than judging by the leaves alone.

Checking soil moisture: Insert a finger about two inches into the soil, or use
a moisture meter. For most houseplants, water when the top inch or two of soil
has dried out. Succulents and cacti prefer the soil to dry out almost
completely between waterings. Ferns and many tropical foliage plants prefer
consistently moist (but not soggy) soil.

Signs of overwatering: Leaves turn yellow and feel soft or mushy, the soil
smells sour or like rot, and the plant may wilt even though the soil is wet.
Roots deprived of oxygen for extended periods begin to rot, which can spread
quickly if not addressed. If overwatering is suspected, let the soil dry out
more than usual before the next watering, and check that the pot has drainage
holes so water isn't pooling at the bottom.

Signs of underwatering: Leaves become dry, crispy, and may curl inward. Growth
slows and the plant may look wilted but perk back up shortly after watering.
Soil pulls away from the sides of the pot when severely dry.

General watering technique: Water thoroughly until it runs out of the
drainage holes, rather than giving small frequent sips that only wet the
surface. This encourages roots to grow downward and access water more
efficiently. Empty any saucer that collects excess water within 30 minutes so
roots aren't sitting in standing water.

Seasonal adjustment: Most plants need less water in cooler months when growth
slows, and more water during their active growing season (typically spring and
summer for most houseplants). Adjust the watering schedule rather than
following a fixed calendar interval year-round.
