SOIL, REPOTTING, AND FEEDING

Choosing a soil mix: Different plant types benefit from different drainage
characteristics. Succulents and cacti do best in a fast-draining, gritty mix
(often a standard potting mix amended with extra perlite, pumice, or coarse
sand). Tropical foliage plants generally do well in a well-draining but
moisture-retentive mix, often amended with perlite or orchid bark. Ferns
typically prefer a mix that holds a bit more moisture, sometimes amended with
coco coir or peat. Vegetables and flowering plants usually do well in a
standard, nutrient-rich potting or garden soil with good drainage.

When to repot: Common signs a plant has outgrown its pot include roots
circling densely at the bottom or emerging from drainage holes, water running
straight through without being absorbed, and noticeably slowed growth despite
otherwise good care. Most actively growing houseplants benefit from repotting
every one to two years; slower growers may go two to three years or more.
Spring, at the start of the active growing season, is generally the best time
to repot, since the plant recovers from root disturbance faster.

Fertilizing basics: Fertilizers are usually labeled with three numbers (the
N-P-K ratio) representing nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium content.
Nitrogen supports leafy green growth, phosphorus supports root and flower
development, and potassium supports overall plant health and stress
tolerance. Most houseplants benefit from a balanced, diluted liquid fertilizer
every two to four weeks during the active growing season, and little to no
fertilizer during dormancy in winter. Over-fertilizing can cause fertilizer
burn, visible as browned leaf tips and edges, so it's safer to under-dilute
than over-dilute when in doubt.

Common deficiency symptoms: Uniform yellowing of older, lower leaves often
points to a nitrogen deficiency. Yellowing between the veins while the veins
stay green (interveinal chlorosis), especially on new growth, often points to
an iron or magnesium deficiency. A general slow-down in growth combined with
weak stems can suggest an overall lack of nutrients or a soil that has been
depleted after years without repotting or feeding.
